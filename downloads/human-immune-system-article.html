How an immune response unfolds

  1. A barrier blocks entry or traps an organism.
  2. Innate sensors detect microbial patterns or tissue damage.
  3. Inflammatory signals recruit immune cells and activate local defences.
  4. Antigen-presenting cells help activate matching T cells.
  5. B and T lymphocytes expand and develop specialised functions.
  6. Pathogens are neutralised or infected cells removed; repair and regulation follow.
  7. Some lymphocytes persist as memory cells.
CHAPTER 01 OF 20

Introduction: the body's living defence network

Core concept

The immune system is not a single organ or a simple shield. It is a coordinated network of cells, tissues, organs, proteins and chemical signals that continually monitors the body. Its work includes preventing microbes from entering, detecting organisms or substances that have crossed a barrier, removing damaged cells, controlling inflammation and building memory of earlier encounters. It must be powerful enough to control dangerous infections yet restrained enough to avoid unnecessary injury to healthy tissues. This balance is central to health.

Why it matters

Understanding this chapter helps explain why symptoms, laboratory tests and treatments cannot be interpreted in isolation. Immune pathways overlap, and the same cell or signal may have different effects in different tissues. For example, inflammation can help contain infection but can also contribute to tissue injury if it is excessive or prolonged. The goal of a healthy response is not maximal activation; it is an effective, appropriately controlled response followed by recovery.

CHAPTER 02 OF 20

The first line of defence: skin, mucosa and protective chemistry

Core concept

The body's external surfaces are active biological barriers. Intact skin provides a physical boundary supported by keratin, closely joined cells, oils and a resident microbial community. Mucous membranes line the respiratory, digestive and genitourinary tracts. Mucus traps particles; cilia move material out of airways; tears and saliva wash surfaces and contain antimicrobial substances. Stomach acidity, digestive enzymes and antimicrobial peptides create environments that make survival difficult for many microbes. These barriers do not sterilise the body; they reduce the chance that organisms reach vulnerable tissues.

CHAPTER 03 OF 20

The lymphatic system: vessels, lymph and immune checkpoints

Core concept

Blood capillaries continually exchange fluid with tissues. Much of the excess interstitial fluid is collected by lymphatic capillaries and returned to the circulation as lymph. Lymphatic vessels contain valves that help maintain one-way flow. Along these vessels, lymph nodes act as organised meeting places where antigens, antigen-presenting cells and lymphocytes can encounter one another. Lymphatic drainage also helps maintain fluid balance and transports immune cells. The spleen performs a related surveillance role for blood-borne material rather than lymph arriving from a local tissue.

CHAPTER 04 OF 20

Primary and secondary lymphoid organs

Core concept

Bone marrow and thymus are primary lymphoid organs: they support the development and maturation of lymphocytes. B cells mature in bone marrow; T-cell precursors travel to the thymus, where selection processes help establish a repertoire capable of recognising foreign antigens while limiting dangerous self-reactivity. Secondary lymphoid organs include lymph nodes, spleen and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues. They provide structured environments where mature lymphocytes can meet antigens and become activated. Tonsils, adenoids, Peyer's patches and other mucosal tissues help monitor surfaces exposed to the environment.

CHAPTER 05 OF 20

The innate immune system: rapid recognition and response

Core concept

Innate immunity responds rapidly to infection or tissue damage. Cells use pattern-recognition receptors to detect molecular features commonly found in microbes or released by injured cells. These signals can trigger cytokine production, inflammation, antimicrobial activity and recruitment of additional immune cells. Innate responses are not simply crude or non-specific: they recognise broad classes of danger and shape the adaptive response that follows. Skin cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, neutrophils, natural killer cells and soluble proteins all contribute.

CHAPTER 06 OF 20

Phagocytes: neutrophils, monocytes and macrophages

Core concept

Phagocytosis is the process by which a cell surrounds, internalises and breaks down particles such as microbes or cellular debris. Neutrophils are abundant circulating white blood cells that can be recruited quickly to sites of acute inflammation. Monocytes circulate in blood and can differentiate into macrophage-like cells in tissues. Macrophages engulf material, release signalling molecules, clear dead cells and help coordinate repair. Their behaviour depends on tissue context; they are not merely disposable microbe-eating cells.

CHAPTER 07 OF 20

Dendritic cells and antigen presentation

Core concept

Dendritic cells are specialised sentinels that capture material in tissues and present processed antigen fragments to T lymphocytes. They connect innate detection with adaptive specificity. Antigen presentation involves peptide fragments displayed by major histocompatibility complex molecules. The context of presentation, co-stimulatory signals and cytokines influences whether T cells activate and what type of response develops. This helps explain why the immune system responds differently to infection, harmless environmental material and the body's own tissues.

CHAPTER 08 OF 20

Natural killer cells and antiviral surveillance

Core concept

Natural killer cells are innate lymphocytes that can detect and eliminate certain stressed, infected or abnormal cells. They integrate activating and inhibitory signals rather than relying on the same antigen-specific receptors used by T cells. Some viruses alter normal cell-surface signals; NK cells can respond to these changes. They release cytotoxic granules that help trigger target-cell death and produce cytokines that influence other immune cells. NK cells complement, rather than replace, adaptive cytotoxic T-cell responses.

CHAPTER 09 OF 20

The adaptive immune system: specificity and clonal expansion

Core concept

Adaptive immunity is built around B and T lymphocytes with highly specific antigen receptors. A lymphocyte whose receptor recognises a relevant antigen can become activated when the appropriate additional signals are present. It then proliferates, producing a clone of cells with related specificity. Some become effector cells that act during the current response; others become memory cells that can respond more rapidly after later exposure. This system takes time to develop during a first encounter but can provide highly targeted protection.

CHAPTER 10 OF 20

B cells, plasma cells and antibodies

Core concept

B lymphocytes can bind antigen through their surface immunoglobulin receptors. With appropriate activation signals, some B cells differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies, while others become memory B cells. Antibodies are proteins with variable regions that bind particular molecular structures. Depending on their class and context, antibodies can neutralise toxins or viruses, mark targets for phagocytosis, activate complement or help protect mucosal surfaces. Antibody levels and protective effects vary over time and between infections and vaccines.

T lymphocytes: helper, cytotoxic and regulatory roles

Core concept

T cells mature in the thymus and recognise antigen fragments presented by major histocompatibility complex molecules. CD4 helper T cells coordinate immune responses through cell contact and cytokines; different helper subsets support different kinds of defence. CD8 cytotoxic T cells can kill infected or abnormal cells displaying appropriate antigen. Regulatory T cells help restrain immune activity and maintain tolerance. These roles overlap and are shaped by the tissue environment, antigen, co-stimulation and signals from other cells.

Cytokines, complement and chemical communication

Core concept

Immune cells coordinate through cytokines, chemokines, receptors and direct contact. Cytokines can activate cells, alter their behaviour, promote growth or help recruit them to a particular tissue. Chemokines guide cell movement along concentration gradients. The complement system is a group of circulating proteins that can coat microbes, promote inflammation and in some circumstances help damage microbial membranes. These systems amplify defence, but their activity must be controlled because excessive or misplaced activation can injure host tissues.

CHAPTER 13 OF 20

Inflammation, fever and tissue repair

Core concept

Inflammation is a protective response to infection or injury. Local blood vessels change their permeability and flow; chemical signals recruit immune cells; and the affected area may become warm, swollen, red and painful. Fever is a regulated rise in body temperature driven by signalling pathways that influence the brain's temperature-control centre. Both inflammation and fever can support defence, but neither is automatically proof of a bacterial infection. Resolution is an active process involving removal of debris, regulatory signals and tissue repair.

CHAPTER 14 OF 20

How the body responds to viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites

Core concept

Different pathogens create different challenges. Many bacteria multiply outside cells and may produce toxins; others live inside host cells. Viruses depend on host-cell machinery and require responses that include interferons, antibodies and cytotoxic lymphocytes. Fungi have distinctive cell walls and can cause superficial or invasive disease, particularly when host defences are impaired. Parasites range from microscopic protozoa to multicellular worms and can require specialised antibody, eosinophil and tissue responses. No single immune mechanism protects equally against every pathogen.

CHAPTER 15 OF 20

Immunological memory and why repeat exposure can differ

Core concept

After an adaptive response, some B and T cells persist as memory populations. On re-exposure to a related antigen, these cells may respond faster or more strongly than naïve cells. Memory is not a guarantee of complete or lifelong protection: pathogens can change, immunity can wane, and protection against infection may differ from protection against severe disease. Immune memory is one reason previous infection or vaccination can change the course of a later encounter.

CHAPTER 16 OF 20

Vaccines: training immune memory safely

Core concept

Vaccines expose the immune system to an antigen or instructions for making an antigen, without requiring the person to experience the full disease. The precise platform differs: vaccines may use inactivated or weakened organisms, purified components, viral vectors or nucleic-acid instructions. Antigen-presenting cells and lymphocytes coordinate the response, and memory cells can develop. Schedules, boosters and recommendations depend on the vaccine, age, health status, exposure risk and national guidance. Vaccination decisions should be based on reliable clinical advice, not claims of instant or universal immunity.

CHAPTER 17 OF 20

The gut microbiome and mucosal immunity

Core concept

The digestive tract contains a diverse community of microorganisms that interact with food, the intestinal barrier and immune cells. These communities can contribute to digestion, produce metabolites and influence immune development and regulation. The relationship is complex: effects vary by species, diet, age, medication and health context. The gut-associated lymphoid tissue samples material from the intestinal environment while maintaining tolerance to many harmless foods and resident microbes. No single supplement or food can be said to 'boost' immunity for everyone.

How the process works in the body

The digestive tract contains a diverse community of microorganisms that interact with food, the intestinal barrier and immune cells. These communities can contribute to digestion, produce metabolites and influence immune development and regulation. The relationship is complex: effects vary by species, diet, age, medication and health context. The gut-associated lymphoid tissue samples material from the intestinal environment while maintaining tolerance to many harmless foods and resident microbes. No single supplement or food can be said to 'boost' immunity for everyone. The response depends on the location, the type of threat and the condition of the surrounding tissue. Cells communicate through receptor binding and soluble mediators. Some signals increase activity, while others limit it or help the response resolve. This coordination allows immune defence to be local when possible and systemic when necessary.

Why it matters

Understanding this chapter helps explain why symptoms, laboratory tests and treatments cannot be interpreted in isolation. Immune pathways overlap, and the same cell or signal may have different effects in different tissues. For example, inflammation can help contain infection but can also contribute to tissue injury if it is excessive or prolonged. The goal of a healthy response is not maximal activation; it is an effective, appropriately controlled response followed by recovery.

CHAPTER 18 OF 20

Allergies, autoimmunity and immune dysregulation

Core concept

An allergic response occurs when the immune system reacts to an otherwise harmless substance. In many immediate allergies, IgE antibodies and mast cells contribute to symptoms that can range from mild to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Autoimmune diseases involve immune responses directed against the body's own components, with varied mechanisms and organs affected. Immunodeficiency occurs when one or more components of immune defence are absent or impaired. These conditions are diverse and require proper medical assessment; they are not evidence that the immune system is simply 'too strong' or 'too weak' in every respect.

Immune health across the lifespan

Core concept

Immune function changes with age. Early life involves development and education of immune responses; pregnancy involves specialised regulation; adulthood reflects accumulated exposures and vaccination; and older age can bring changes in immune-cell production, response quality and inflammation. Nutrition, sleep, physical activity, chronic disease, medications and environmental exposures can influence health, but effects are individual. Healthy habits support general wellbeing; they do not replace vaccination, diagnosis or treatment.

Core concept

Everyday immune health: what evidence supports

General health measures—adequate sleep, balanced nutrition, regular physical activity, recommended vaccination, hand hygiene and avoiding tobacco—support health and reduce some infection risks. They do not make a person immune to every disease. No food, detox, supplement or product can reliably 'supercharge' the immune system. Supplements can interact with medicines and may be harmful in excess; discuss them with a qualified clinician when needed.

When to seek medical advice

Repeated, unusually severe or persistent infections, unexplained symptoms, severe allergic reactions, or concerns about vaccine timing deserve professional assessment. Difficulty breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, collapse or signs of a severe allergic reaction require emergency care. This encyclopedia is for education and does not diagnose conditions or replace a clinician's advice.
